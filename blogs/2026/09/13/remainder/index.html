AI proved a math conjecture I made as an undergraduate

2026-09-13

The problem

Given positive integers nnn and kkk with k<nk<nk<n, consider n−1n-1n−1 balls labelled
1,2,…,n−11,2,\ldots,n-11,2,…,n−1. Color them subject to the following rules:

  1. Balls iii and n−in-in−i have the same color.
  2. Balls iii and ∣i−k∣|i-k|∣i−k∣ have the same color whenever i≠ki\ne ki=k.

What is the maximum possible number of colors?

I recently rediscovered this problem in my undergraduate notes. I proposed this problem “inspired by a homework problem in my discrete mathematics
class.” At the time, I experimentally found what appeared to be the answer, but I
never managed to prove it rigorously. Years later, I gave the problem to an AI,
which revealed the simple algebraic structure behind the conjecture.

My original observation

Let f(n,k)f(n,k)f(n,k) denote the answer. From small examples, I observed the recurrence

f(n,k)=f(max⁡(n−k,k),min⁡(n−k,k)).f(n,k)=f\bigl(\max(n-k,k),\min(n-k,k)\bigr). f(n,k)=f(max(n−k,k),min(n−k,k)).

This resembles the Euclidean algorithm, so I conjectured that the answer depends
only on gcd⁡(n,k)\gcd(n,k)gcd(n,k). More precisely, if d=gcd⁡(n,k)d=\gcd(n,k)d=gcd(n,k), then

f(n,k)=⌊d2⌋+1.\boxed{f(n,k)=\left\lfloor\frac d2\right\rfloor+1.} f(n,k)=⌊2d​⌋+1.​

The conjecture is correct and proved by AI. Following are AI-generated contents.

Proof

Think of each ball as a vertex. Join two vertices whenever the rules require the
corresponding balls to have the same color. Every connected component must be
monochromatic, while different components may receive different colors. Thus,
f(n,k)f(n,k)f(n,k) is exactly the number of connected components.

It is convenient to regard the labels as the nonzero elements of
Z/nZ\mathbb Z/n\mathbb ZZ/nZ. The first rule says

i∼−i(modn).i\sim-i\pmod n. i∼−i(modn).

The second rule, together with the first, gives an edge between iii and
i−k(modn)i-k\pmod ni−k(modn). If i>ki>ki>k, this follows directly from i∼i−ki\sim i-ki∼i−k. If i<ki<ki<k,
then

i∼k−i∼−(k−i)=i−k(modn).i\sim k-i\sim -(k-i)=i-k\pmod n. i∼k−i∼−(k−i)=i−k(modn).

The relation ∼\sim∼ is symmetric: if we can move from iii to i−ki-ki−k, we can
traverse the same edge backward and move from i−ki-ki−k to iii. Thus we may both
subtract and add kkk. Repeating these moves, and applying negation when desired,
produces labels congruent to

±i+qk(modn),q∈Z.\pm i+qk\pmod n,\qquad q\in\mathbb Z. ±i+qk(modn),q∈Z.

Equivalently, one could take only nonnegative qqq: because the sequence is
periodic modulo nnn, a negative value of qqq can always be replaced by a
nonnegative one. The only caveat is that 000 is not an actual label; we handle
that case below.

Now let d=gcd⁡(n,k)d=\gcd(n,k)d=gcd(n,k). The subgroup of Z/nZ\mathbb Z/n\mathbb ZZ/nZ generated by kkk
is precisely the set of multiples of ddd. Therefore, repeated addition of kkk
can change a label by any multiple of ddd, and negation can change its residue
modulo ddd from rrr to −r-r−r. It follows that two labels iii and jjj are connected
exactly when

j≡i(modd)orj≡−i(modd).j\equiv i\pmod d \qquad\text{or}\qquad j\equiv-i\pmod d. j≡i(modd)orj≡−i(modd).

n−kn-kn−k; the first coloring rule connects those two endpoints. Hence all nonzero
multiples of ddd remain in one component.

We are therefore left to count the residue classes modulo ddd after identifying
each residue rrr with −r-r−r. They are

{0},{1,d−1},{2,d−2},….\{0\},\{1,d-1\},\{2,d-2\},\ldots. {0},{1,d−1},{2,d−2},….

1+(d−2)/2+11+(d-2)/2+11+(d−2)/2+1 because both 000 and d/2d/2d/2 are fixed by negation. In either case,

⌊d2⌋+1.\left\lfloor\frac d2\right\rfloor+1. ⌊2d​⌋+1.

The hidden structure

There are two complementary ways to describe the structure behind the problem.
The first is concrete and gives the shortest proof; the second places the same
argument in a broader group-theoretic setting.

The cyclic-group interpretation

Regard the labels as elements of the cyclic group Z/nZ\mathbb Z/n\mathbb ZZ/nZ. The
coloring rules allow the operations

i⟼i+kandi⟼−i.i\longmapsto i+k \qquad\text{and}\qquad i\longmapsto-i. i⟼i+kandi⟼−i.

The subgroup generated by kkk consists precisely of the multiples of
d=gcd⁡(n,k)d=\gcd(n,k)d=gcd(n,k). Translation by kkk therefore moves within a residue class modulo
ddd, while negation identifies the class rrr with −r-r−r. Consequently, the
connected components are indexed by

(Z/dZ)/{r∼−r}.(\mathbb Z/d\mathbb Z)/\{r\sim-r\}. (Z/dZ)/{r∼−r}.

Counting these classes immediately gives ⌊d/2⌋+1\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1⌊d/2⌋+1. This is the
most direct interpretation of why the gcd appears in the answer.

The dihedral-group interpretation

Alternatively, write the two original operations as reflections:

s0(i)=−i,s1(i)=k−i(modn).s_0(i)=-i, \qquad s_1(i)=k-i \pmod n. s0​(i)=−i,s1​(i)=k−i(modn).

Their composition is the translation

s1s0(i)=i+k.s_1s_0(i)=i+k. s1​s0​(i)=i+k.

Abstractly, two reflections of this form generate the infinite dihedral group
D∞D_\inftyD∞​, equivalently the affine Weyl group of type A~1\widetilde A_1A1​. The
orbit of iii under its action on Z/nZ\mathbb Z/n\mathbb ZZ/nZ is

{ ±i+qk(modn):q∈Z }.\{\,\pm i+qk\pmod n:q\in\mathbb Z\,\}. {±i+qk(modn):q∈Z}.

The coloring graph is essentially the Schreier graph of this action: applying
either generator draws an edge, so its connected components are exactly the
orbits. Although D∞D_\inftyD∞​ is infinite, this particular action is finite. The
translation i↦i+ki\mapsto i+ki↦i+k has order n/dn/dn/d, so the action factors through a
finite dihedral group.

Thus the two descriptions say the same thing at different levels. The
cyclic-group view computes the orbits directly; the dihedral-group view explains
why translation and reflection occur together. The gcd formula is the concrete
shadow of this underlying dihedral symmetry.
